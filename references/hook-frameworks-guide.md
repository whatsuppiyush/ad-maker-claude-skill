# Hook Frameworks Guide — why hooks work

A hook is the first line, first frame, or first 1-3 seconds of an ad: the part responsible for
stopping the scroll. Everything after it only matters if the hook buys the attention to get
there. This is the craft behind the templates in `data/hook-frameworks.md`.

## The one job of a hook

Not to sell. Not to explain. **To earn the next three seconds.** A viewer decides in about a
second and a half whether to keep watching. The hook has one job: make scrolling past feel like
missing something. Sell later; survive the first second first.

## The mechanisms that stop a scroll

### 1. The curiosity gap
The brain hates an open loop. When a hook implies "there's an answer coming and you don't have it
yet," the viewer stays to close the gap. The trick is a gap that's **specific enough to feel
real** but **open enough to require watching.** "Here's a marketing tip" has no gap. "The one
setting that was quietly killing your reach" has one.

### 2. Pattern interrupt
Feeds train the eye to skim. Anything that breaks the expected rhythm — an abrupt cold open, an
unexpected visual, a line that sounds nothing like an ad — makes the thumb pause. This is why
"native"-looking ads outperform polished ones on TikTok and Reels: the polish *is* the pattern
the viewer is scrolling past.

### 3. Self-identification
"That's me." When a viewer recognizes their own exact situation — the specific symptom, the
specific frustration, the specific identity — they stop, because the ad is suddenly about them.
Precision is what triggers it. "Busy people" identifies no one; "you reopen the same five tabs
every morning and still forget the one thing" identifies someone.

### 4. Specificity as credibility
Specific numbers, timeframes, and details read as true; round, vague claims read as marketing.
"Grew fast" is ignored. "Went from 400 to 3,100 in six weeks" is believed (only ever use real
figures). Specificity does double duty: it stops the scroll *and* it earns trust.

### 5. Tension and stakes
A hook with something at risk — a cost of inaction, a mistake being made right now, a deadline —
creates a small emotional charge the viewer wants to resolve. No tension, no reason to stay.

## The first second is visual, not just verbal

On video, half the hook is what's on screen before anyone reads a word:
- **The opening frame** — motion, a face, an unexpected object. A static talking-head start loses
  to a hand already mid-action.
- **On-screen text** — most feeds autoplay on mute. The hook has to land as text too. Put the
  hook line on screen in the first frame, big and legible.
- **The first spoken words** — no "hey guys, welcome back." Start on the hook. The intro is where
  viewers leave.

## Writing a strong hook (the checklist)

- **Lead with the sharpest element** — the number, the claim, the pain, the question. Never bury
  it behind a warm-up.
- **One idea.** A hook that carries two ideas carries none. Cut to the strongest.
- **Concrete over clever.** A pun feels like an ad. A specific detail feels like a person.
- **Say it like a text to a friend**, not a press release. Contractions, short words, real voice.
- **Match the awareness stage** (see `data/angle-matrix.md`). A cold viewer needs the problem
  opened; a warm one needs a reason to act.
- **Make the promise payable.** If the hook opens a loop, the body must close it fast. A hook
  that over-promises and under-delivers trains people to scroll your next ad.

## What kills a hook

- A slow build ("In today's video we're going to talk about...").
- A generic opener that could front any ad in the category.
- Burying the interesting part in sentence three.
- Fake urgency or a fabricated stat — it may stop the scroll once, but it poisons trust and the
  platform's comments will say so.
- Trying to be liked instead of being watched. Safe is invisible.

## How many hooks to test

The hook is the highest-leverage variable in the whole ad — same body, different hook, wildly
different results. Media buyers routinely test **8-15 hook variations** per product before
scaling one. Test across **different families** (`data/hook-frameworks.md`), not ten rewordings
of the same angle — otherwise you learn which wording won, not which *idea* won.
